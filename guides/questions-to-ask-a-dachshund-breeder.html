How do you find a reputable dachshund breeder?

By asking a small number of specific questions and listening to how they are answered rather than what is claimed. Price will not do it for you: the reputable breeder band of $1,000–$3,000 overlaps the online marketplace band of $500–$2,000, so a broker can sit comfortably inside a breeder’s price range. Documentation and a face-to-face sale can tell them apart. A number on a listing cannot.

What health testing should the parents have?

Three things, and it really is only three. The AKC parent-club health testing requirements for the Dachshund are a Patella Evaluation, a Cardiac Exam, and an Ophthalmologist Evaluation. That is the entire list.

Ask for results for both parents, with dates and the examining specialist named, and verify them yourself at ofa.org rather than accepting a photograph of a certificate. A breeder who is doing this work will hand the results over before you finish the sentence. A breeder who says the line is clean, or that the vet checked them over, is describing an opinion rather than a test.

Check OFA yourself

We do not print a CHIC requirement list for this breed, because we could not retrieve and verify the OFA page. Go to ofa.org, search the sire and dam by registered name, and read what is actually recorded. The database is public and free.

Which DNA tests matter?

Commercial panels for the breed cover CDDY/IVDD (FGF4), PRA crd4/cord1 (RPGRIP1), Degenerative Myelopathy, Osteogenesis Imperfecta, LGMD 2D (in miniatures), MPS IIIA, Narcolepsy, NCL1 and NCL2.

One deserves singling out. Lafora disease (NHLRC1) is concentrated in miniature wirehaired dachshunds, the DNA test is established and commercially available, and screening works: UK screening cut the proportion of affected dogs from 10.4% in 2012 to 2.7% in 2017. If you are buying a miniature wirehaired puppy, ask about Lafora status by name. A breeder in that variety who has not heard of it is not following their own variety’s health work.

Why is the CDDY test not a selection tool?

Because nearly every dachshund alive fails it. 93% of dachshunds carry two copies of the CDDY variant, and the allele frequency is 0.98. A test that returns the same answer for almost every dog cannot separate dogs.

Dachshund Health UK states the position directly: "we do not currently recommend the use of the UC Davis DNA Test (CDDY)", warning that relying on it "will have an irreversible effect on the Dachshund breed excluding almost all dogs from breeding." The variant is inseparable from the short legs that define the breed — the same FGF4 retrogene produces both.

"Our dogs are CDDY clear" is a sentence to examine closely

With 93% of the breed carrying two copies, a genuinely clear dachshund is a rarity. Either the claim is wrong, or the breeder is selecting on a marker their own health authorities advise against using for selection. Ask to see the report, and ask what they think it means.

What about back scoring?

This is the real screening tool for disc disease, and it barely exists in America. Radiographic back scoring measures spinal calcification and is used to make breeding decisions. Denmark has run a scheme for around twenty years; since 2019 its rules require both parents to be X-rayed and permit only dogs graded K0 to K2 to breed. The UK’s Royal Kennel Club launched an IVDD scheme in 2021.

In the United States, neither the AKC nor the Dachshund Club of America recommends it, and only about three US breeders have been confirmed to X-ray for spinal calcification. So this is not a question you can use to filter American breeders — almost none will say yes. It is a question worth asking anyway, because the answer tells you how closely they follow the breed’s health research. A breeder who knows what you are asking about and explains why they do not do it is a different proposition from one who has never heard of it.

Does a USDA license mean anything good?

It means the opposite of what sellers imply. USDA and APHIS state that "if you maintain four or fewer breeding females … you do not have to be USDA licensed", and that "if you always sell pets to new owners in face-to-face transactions, you do not have to be USDA licensed, no matter how many breeding females you have." Photos, videos and video calls do not count as face-to-face.

Run that backwards. A breeder holding a USDA license is, by definition, either running more than four breeding females and shipping to buyers sight unseen. The license is a marker of sight-unseen commercial volume. A small hobby breeder who sells face to face has no reason to hold one. "We are USDA licensed and inspected," offered as reassurance, tells you what kind of operation you are talking to.

What does AKC Breeder of Merit prove?

Participation, mostly. The program requires at least five years in AKC events, parent club membership, AKC titles on at least four dogs bred, certification that parent-club health screens are performed, and 100% of puppies AKC registered. Those are real commitments and they filter out drive-by operations. But note the word certification: the breeder attests that the screens are done. It is a participation credential, not an independent health audit. Verify the screens at ofa.org regardless.

What should you ask about color?

Ask what the sire and dam are, and specifically whether either is a dapple. A dapple × dapple mating produces roughly 25% double-dapple puppies, and the Dachshund Club of America states these "are more likely to carry health issues such as deafness, reduced eye size or missing eyes." Red dapples can be visually ambiguous, so accidental dapple-to-dapple matings are a genuine risk rather than a theoretical one — which is why the honest answer to "is either parent a dapple" is a considered one, not a shrug.

The DCA also warns that dilutes — Blue and Isabella — carry "thin coats, skin sensitivity, vaccine failures and reduced lifespan." Colour dilution alopecia is genetic and recessive, dachshunds are explicitly listed as susceptible, the coat is normal at birth with signs from about six months, and it is not curable.

Note the perverse incentive while you are at it. Dapple adds $500–$1,000 to the price of a puppy. The two biggest premiums in the market attach to patterns the breed’s own parent club warns about, which is worth saying plainly to anyone quoting you a colour surcharge.

Which answers should end the conversation?

  • "We do not health test — our line is clean." The three-test list is short and cheap. Not doing it is a choice.
  • "Both parents are dapple," or any version of specialising in double dapples.
  • Refusal or reluctance to let you meet the dam, in person, where the puppies live.
  • "We can ship him this week" for a puppy under eight weeks old.
  • Payment by wire transfer, gift card, cryptocurrency or a peer-to-peer app only.
  • Several litters always available, in every colour, year round.
  • "We are USDA licensed" offered as a quality credential.
  • Pressure — a deposit deadline, another buyer waiting, a discount for deciding today.
  • No contract, or a contract with no health guarantee and no return clause.
  • No questions asked about you. A breeder who does not interview you is not placing puppies, they are selling them.

How do you avoid a puppy scam?

By insisting on a live, in-person meeting before money moves. The BBB Scam Tracker logged 2,899 puppy scam reports in 2023 with a median loss of $700, 2,278 in 2024 at $680, and 1,632 through September 2025 at $600, with individual losses documented between $1,900 and $4,500. Those counts understate the problem badly: the BBB cites FTC data that only 4.8% of mass-market fraud victims report at all, which puts true volume at roughly twenty times the logged figures.

What protection do you have after the sale?

Some, depending on your state and who you bought from. Twenty-two states have puppy lemon laws, typically giving a short window for infectious illness and a longer one for congenital or hereditary defects, with remedies of refund, exchange or reimbursement of veterinary costs. California allows 15 days for illness and one year for congenital or hereditary conditions, with a retention remedy of up to 150% of the purchase price. Florida runs 14 days and one year, Illinois 21 days, Vermont 7.

Two limits matter. These laws bind dealers and retailers, not every hobby breeder, and remedies are usually capped at the purchase price — which will not touch the cost of a serious condition. Separately, ten states now ban retail pet store puppy sales (California was first in 2017, Colorado in 2026, Delaware the tenth), along with 530 localities across 31 states.

Should I buy from a USDA licensed dachshund breeder?

A USDA license is not a quality mark. USDA and APHIS state that breeders with four or fewer breeding females, or who always sell face-to-face, need no license regardless of volume. Holding one therefore indicates larger-scale, sight-unseen selling. Photos, videos and video calls do not count as face-to-face.

No. About 93% of dachshunds carry two copies and the allele frequency is 0.98, so the test returns the same answer for nearly every dog. Dachshund Health UK does not recommend using it for selection, warning it would exclude almost all dogs from breeding. It is not a screening tool for buyers.
